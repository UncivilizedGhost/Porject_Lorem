# Lung Cancer — Findings Summary

Compiled from:
- Finnish Cancer Registry (Syöpärekisteri / THL) quality reports: *keuhkosyoparaportti* (cases 2019–2023) and *Keuhkosyöpä Suomessa* (cases 1.1.2018–30.6.2022)
- Finnish Cancer Registry statistical reports: *Cancer 2022*, *Cancer in Finland 2023*, *Syöpä 2024*
- Patient information: Kaikki syövästä (Syöpäjärjestöt / Finnish Cancer Society), reviewed 2023 by pulmonologist Eeva-Maija Nieminen (HUS)

---

## 1. Epidemiology and Disease Burden (Finland)

| Measure | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Incidence** | Nearly 3,000 new lung cancer cases per year in Finland (~2,880–2,892 in 2023–2024). Third most common cancer overall (after prostate and breast). |
| **Mortality** | Lung cancer is the **most common cause of cancer death annually** for both women and men. |
| **Lifetime risk** | ~3.4% of women and ~5.2% of men develop lung cancer; ~2.7% of women and ~4.7% of men die from it. |
| **Years of life lost** | Largest loss of life years among men (~11.6 average years lost per patient). |
| **Sex trends** | Men's incidence has fallen markedly since the mid-1970s (now below Norway and Denmark, but above Sweden). Women's incidence rose for decades and is only now beginning to decline (expected to turn downward in the early 2030s). |
| **Stage at diagnosis** | Only a small share (~5%) are diagnosed at a localized stage; most are detected after spread, driving poor survival. |
| **5-year relative survival** | Low and among the weakest in the Nordic countries, but improving slightly: men 14% → 16%, women 23% → 26% (comparing 2019–2021 vs 2022–2024). |
| **Social gradient** | The largest education-based differences in incidence/mortality of all cancers — people with lower education have substantially higher lung cancer rates. |

## 2. Risk Factors and Causes

### From the patient-information source (Kaikki syövästä / Syöpäjärjestöt)

- **Smoking is the most common cause** — about **90% of lung cancers** are due to smoking.
- Tobacco smoke contains over 5,000 chemical compounds, at least 60 of which are carcinogenic.
- A smoker's risk of lung cancer is **15–30 times higher** than that of a never-smoker. Risk depends on age of starting, number of cigarettes, and years of smoking.
- About **1 in 10 lung cancer patients has never smoked**.
- **Passive smoking (secondhand smoke)** increases risk **2–3 fold**.
- **Quitting smoking reduces risk**; after 10 years risk is significantly lower, but still higher than in never-smokers.
- **Work and living environment factors** that increase risk: **arsenic, nickel and chromium compounds, radon, and asbestos**. For smokers exposed to these, quitting is especially important — asbestos can raise a smoker's risk up to **50-fold**.
- **COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease)** and previous **tuberculosis** also increase risk.

### From the registry statistical reports (indirect evidence)

The statistical reports do not contain a dedicated risk-factor chapter, but their patterns point to:
- Historical **smoking patterns** as the driver of sex- and time-trends (men quitting earlier → declining male rates; women smoking later → rising then falling female rates).
- **Socioeconomic / education inequality** as a major population-level risk marker.

## 3. Symptoms

Lung cancer is often **symptom-free in early stages**, and a tumor can grow large in certain parts of the lungs before causing symptoms.

### Common first symptoms
- Persistent cough
- Coughing up blood (bloody sputum)
- Shortness of breath
- Chest or shoulder pain
- Hoarseness
- Difficulty swallowing
- Recurrent lung infections

These early symptoms are often not recognized as lung cancer, so people do not seek investigation promptly.

### Symptoms if the cancer has spread (metastasized)
- **General symptoms:** loss of appetite, weakness, fatigue, feverishness, weight loss
- **Pressure on the superior vena cava:** swelling of neck and face, breathing difficulties especially when lying down
- **Liver metastases:** nausea, feeling of fullness under the right rib cage
- **Bone metastases:** pain and aching
- **Brain metastases:** seizures or paralysis symptoms

Symptoms depend on tumor location and where it has spread.

## 4. Diagnosis and Staging

### Diagnostic pathway (typical)
1. **Chest X-ray** — first imaging test; detects tumors larger than ~1 cm.
2. **CT scan** (chest and upper abdomen) — assesses spread, lymph node involvement, and metastases to bones or abdominal organs; sometimes combined with **PET-CT**.
3. **Lung function tests** (spirometry) — assess whether the patient can tolerate biopsy, surgery, or radiotherapy.
4. **Bronchoscopy** — a flexible scope passed through the nose/mouth and trachea into the lungs to view the tumor and take biopsies. Not painful, but may irritate coughing.
5. **Needle biopsy** (fine or core needle through the skin) — used if the tumor is not near the bronchi, depending on tumor location and lung condition.
6. **Blood tests** — there are no specific blood markers for detecting lung cancer; general blood counts and liver/kidney function tests are used to assess overall health.

### Classification
Lung cancer is divided into two main types:

| Type | Share | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC)** | Most cases (>80%) | Includes squamous cell carcinoma, adenocarcinoma, and large cell anaplastic carcinoma. Staged using the **TNM system**. |
| **Small cell lung cancer (SCLC)** | <20% | Spreads early and is almost always disseminated at diagnosis. Classified as **limited disease (LD)** or **extensive disease (ED)**. |

Because the lungs have rich blood and lymph supply, cancer cells spread easily. Common metastasis sites: mediastinal and supraclavicular lymph nodes, liver, bones, brain, and adrenal glands.

### TNM stages (simplified)
- **Stage 0:** Carcinoma in situ (Tis N0 M0)
- **Stage I:** Localized tumor, no lymph node spread
- **Stage II:** Local spread to intrapulmonary/hilar lymph nodes, or larger tumor without node spread
- **Stage III:** Locally advanced disease
- **Stage IV:** Distant metastases (IVa / IVb depending on extent)

## 5. Treatment

Treatment depends on cancer type, stage, patient's general condition, and lung function. Often a combination of approaches is used. If the patient is elderly or in poor condition, symptom management may be more beneficial than active cancer treatment. For widespread disease, treatments aim to prolong life and relieve symptoms rather than cure.

### Surgery
- The **only potentially curative treatment**.
- Used for non-small cell lung cancer when possible (squamous cell, adenocarcinoma, large cell carcinoma).
- Only about **one-quarter of lung cancers** are operable, because most have already spread at diagnosis.
- Small cell lung cancer is rarely operable due to early spread.
- Requires adequate general condition, lung function, and heart function; the tumor must be sufficiently localized.
- Procedures: pneumectomy (whole lung), lobectomy (one lobe), bilobectomy (two lobes), plus removal of local lymph nodes; often done via thoracoscopy (VATS) rather than open chest surgery.
- If cancer cells are found in lymph nodes or the tumor is large, **adjuvant chemotherapy** may follow surgery.

### Radiotherapy
- For patients who cannot be operated on but have sufficient general condition, lung function, and disease extent.
- Aim: cure or prolong life. Typically 4–6 weeks, small daily doses.
- **Stereotactic radiotherapy (täsmäsädehoito):** for small tumors without spread, usually 5 or fewer sessions with high precise doses; an alternative to surgery when surgical risk is high.
- **Chemoradiation:** chemotherapy combined with radiotherapy for locally advanced non-small cell cancer (stage III) or limited small cell cancer. Intensive, with many side effects; only for patients in good condition.
- **Palliative radiotherapy:** shorter course to relieve symptoms such as bloody cough, shortness of breath, or bone pain.
- Brain metastases may also be irradiated (with steroids to manage swelling).

### Chemotherapy (cytostatics)
- **Primary treatment for small cell lung cancer** (commonly etoposide with cisplatin or carboplatin). Often shrinks tumors initially, but relapse or loss of effect is common.
- Also the **primary treatment for widespread non-small cell lung cancer**; prolongs life, relieves symptoms, improves quality of life. Usually platinum-based combinations; immunotherapy is often added.
- Older/frail patients may receive a single drug due to side effects.
- Side effects: low blood cell counts (fatigue, infection/bleeding risk), nausea (now well-controlled), appetite loss; mostly temporary.

### Targeted medicines (täsmälääkkeet)
- Used only for a small share of patients whose tumors have specific molecular changes (e.g., EGFR activating mutations; ALK, ROS1, etc. rearrangements).
- Examples: gefitinib, erlotinib, afatinib, osimertinib (EGFR); crizotinib, alectinib, lorlatinib, ceritinib, brigatinib (ALK).
- Can provide significantly longer progression-free survival than chemotherapy.
- Used for advanced non-small cell lung cancer of adenocarcinoma type when the tumor tissue shows the relevant genetic change.

### Immunotherapy
- Established for advanced non-small cell lung cancer.
- Drugs: atezolizumab, nivolumab, pembrolizumab, sintilimab, durvalumab — alone or combined with chemotherapy.
- Works by directing the patient's own immune cells to kill cancer cells.
- Some patients have long-lasting responses; others get no response at all.
- Side effects differ from chemotherapy — autoimmune-like reactions (most commonly thyroid dysfunction and various organ inflammations).

## 6. Prognosis and Survival

- After treatment, patients attend follow-up visits to manage side effects, monitor symptoms, and support smoking cessation.
- After surgery: follow-up every 3–6 months for the first 2 years (depending on stage), then annually.
- About **half of surgically treated lung cancers recur**, most within the first 2 years. About **70% of recurrences are distant metastases**, which are not curable.
- For advanced disease, treatment aims at symptom relief and life prolongation. Chemotherapy typically gives about 4–6 months of disease-free time; targeted therapy can give 6–12 months or longer; immunotherapy can yield even longer disease-free periods or years in a small share of patients whose tumors clearly shrink.
- Prognosis varies widely and depends on stage, age, sex, and general symptoms (weight loss) before treatment.
- **Women have a somewhat better prognosis than men**, partly because they have less small cell lung cancer (worst prognosis).
- Metastases to the central nervous system, bones, or liver worsen prognosis.
- **5-year survival: about 13% of men and 21% of women** from diagnosis.
- Survival has improved somewhat in recent years due to targeted treatments.

### First-year survival by first treatment (registry quality report, 2019–2023)
- Curative treatment: 84% alive at 12 months
- Palliative/life-prolonging treatment: 40%
- Symptomatic care: 12%
- No treatment records: 22%
- Overall 1-year mortality: **60% of patients died during the first follow-up year.**
- 1-year survival was significantly better for **women (47%) than men (37%)**.

## 7. Care Quality and Regional Variation (Registry Quality Reports)

### Data source and cohort
- *Keuhkosyöpä Suomessa*: new primary lung cancers (ICD-10 C33–C34), 1.1.2018–30.6.2022, n = 13,324; follow-up 6 months.
- *keuhkosyoparaportti*: new primary lung cancers (ICD-10 C33–C34), 1.1.2019–31.12.2023, n = 14,873; follow-up up to 12 months.
- Data from the Finnish Cancer Registry (SYRE) and the HILMO treatment register. Only each patient's first lung cancer included.

### Patient profile
- About **two-thirds of patients were 70+ years old** at diagnosis; only ~9–10% under 60. Median age ~73.
- About **40% of patients were women** (lowest in Eastern Finland at 35%, highest in Southern Finland at 45%).
- Most new cases in the **Southern Finland** cooperative area; fewest in **Northern Finland**.
- Tumor location unclear in ~40%, upper lobe ~28–30%, lower lobe ~20%.

### Histological type and diagnosis
- **Adenocarcinoma:** ~36% (most common; 45% in under-60s, 27% in over-80s)
- **Squamous cell carcinoma:** ~19% (more common in men)
- **Small cell lung cancer:** 13% (under 20%; ~6% in over-80s)
- **Cell type unknown/undetermined:** over 20–22% of new lung cancers
- **No histological or cytological confirmation:** most common in Northern and Eastern Finland (23% and 19%)
- **Diagnosis method:** histological tissue sample ~60–73%, cytology ~8%, clinical/imaging only ~20–32% (highest in oldest patients)

### Staging data gaps
- **Extent/staging data missing for nearly half (48%) of patients.**
- TNM/stage reported for about one-third of patients; registry's own crude extent classification only for about one-fifth.
- Most often missing in Eastern Finland; most complete in Northern Finland. No significant change over the five-year period.

### MDT (multidisciplinary team) documentation
- MDT records (procedure codes WZC15/WZC20) found mainly **only in the Sisä-Suomi cooperative area (33% of patients)**; practically unavailable elsewhere nationally.
- HILMO only captures data from direct patient contacts, so MDT meeting documentation is poorly captured.

### Treatment within the first year (2019–2023 cohort)
- **Curative treatment** (surgery or curative radiotherapy): **~27%** (4,009 patients)
- **Palliative/life-prolonging treatment:** **~30%** (4,522 patients)
- **Symptomatic/palliative care:** **~40%** (5,871 patients)
- **No treatment records within a year:** **~27.5% (4,089 patients)** — of these, **79% died** within the year.

### Treatment within the first 6 months (2018–mid-2022 cohort)
- Surgery: 11%
- Medication: 15%
- Radiotherapy: 18%
- Palliative care: 12%
- No treatment recorded: 16%
- Death: 28%
- Nearly one-third of patients died within 6 months without any preceding treatment.

### Regional differences
Significant regional differences in diagnostics, treatment decision data, and treatment pathways:
- Curative treatment below national average in **Northern Finland**; palliative/symptomatic shares higher in **Western Finland**.
- Unknown cell type ("clinical cancers") significantly more common in **Northern and Eastern Finland** (28% and 26%) than in **Western Finland** (15%).
- Surgery rates highest in **Sisä-Suomi** (16%), radiotherapy highest in **Etelä-Suomi** (26%), medication highest in **Sisä-Suomi** (31%).
- Palliative care share highest in **Eastern Finland** (29%).
- The report concludes these differences are **not explained by patient structure alone**, but also by differences in diagnostics, treatment processes, and recording practices.

### National Lung Cancer Program goals
- Smooth, high-quality treatment chains
- Increase curative treatment by 10%
- Improve 5-year survival by 5%
- Advanced through quality data use, treatment recommendations (FICAN), regional care chains, and multidisciplinary cooperation (MDT).
